# Level 2 — Elementary (21–40)

## Dictation 56 — An Email of Complaint

## Dictation 57 — The Night Sky
On a clear night, the sky can reveal thousands of stars that are difficult to see in a brightly lit city. Some stars appear brighter because they are closer to Earth or produce more light. Astronomers use powerful telescopes to study distant objects, but even a simple pair of binoculars can make the night sky more interesting.

## Dictation 58 — Learning from Different Cultures
Travelling gives people an opportunity to experience customs that may be unfamiliar to them. In some countries, arriving exactly on time is considered essential, while in others, social events may begin later than announced. Visitors should observe local behaviour and ask polite questions rather than assume that their own habits are universal.

## Dictation 59 — The Rescue Centre
The animal rescue centre cares for abandoned and injured animals until they can be placed in suitable homes. Volunteers prepare food, clean the living areas, and help visitors choose a pet responsibly. The centre also teaches children about animal welfare. It depends on donations, but time and practical support are equally valuable.

## Dictation 60 — A Decision About the Future
Choosing a future career can feel difficult, especially when there are many possible directions. Some students follow the advice of their families, while others prefer to explore their own interests. It is useful to research different professions, speak to experienced people, and consider the skills each job requires. A thoughtful decision does not have to be made in a single day.

## Dictation 76 — The Changing Workplace
Remote work has changed how many organisations communicate and manage projects. Employees may have greater flexibility, but they also need to organise their time and maintain clear boundaries between work and personal life. Managers must ensure that remote staff receive useful feedback and have equal opportunities to contribute. Technology makes distance less significant, but it does not remove the need for trust.

The discovery may provide valuable evidence about the area's early history.

## Dictation 80 — The Importance of Reliable Information
Information travels quickly, particularly through social media, where a dramatic claim can reach thousands of people within minutes. Speed, however, does not guarantee accuracy. Before sharing a story, readers should check its original source, publication date, and supporting evidence. Comparing reports from reliable organisations can help prevent misunderstandings and reduce the spread of false information.

## Dictation 81 — The Limits of First Impressions
First impressions can influence our opinions long before we have enough information to make a fair judgement. A confident speaker may appear knowledgeable, while a quiet person may be wrongly considered uninterested. These assumptions are not always accurate. Taking time to observe someone's behaviour across different situations can produce a more balanced understanding.

## Dictation 82 — The Future of Urban Transport
As cities become more crowded, transport planners are exploring ways to move people efficiently without increasing pollution. Electric buses, protected cycling lanes, and reliable railway networks may reduce dependence on private cars. Nevertheless, introducing new systems requires careful investment and public support. A transport policy that works in one city may not be suitable for another.

## Dictation 83 — A Scientific Explanation
The human brain does not store every experience as a perfect recording. Instead, memories are reconstructed when we recall them, and details may change over time. This helps explain why two people can remember the same event differently. Memory is influenced by attention, emotion, later information, and the questions people are asked.

## Dictation 84 — The Art of Negotiation
Effective negotiation is not simply a matter of persuading another person to accept your proposal. It involves identifying shared interests, understanding the other side's concerns, and exploring possible compromises. Preparation is important, but flexibility is equally valuable. When participants feel that their views have been taken seriously, they are more likely to support the final agreement.

## Dictation 85 — The Digital Archive
The university has launched a digital archive containing thousands of historical documents, photographs, and recorded interviews. Researchers can search the collection by date, location, or subject. Although online access makes the material easier to explore, the archive team must also consider copyright, privacy, and the long-term preservation of digital files.

## Dictation 86 — The Economics of Attention
In the digital economy, attention has become a valuable resource. Many platforms are designed to encourage users to remain online for as long as possible, often by presenting a continuous stream of personalised content. This can be convenient, but it may also interrupt concentration. Developing deliberate habits, such as setting limits and disabling unnecessary notifications, can help people use technology more thoughtfully.

## Dictation 87 — A Conservation Challenge
Conservation projects often involve difficult choices. Protecting a natural habitat may restrict activities that local communities depend on for income, while allowing unrestricted development can cause lasting environmental damage. Successful programmes therefore require more than scientific knowledge. They must also involve residents, consider economic realities, and establish goals that can be maintained over time.

## Dictation 89 — The Problem with Assumptions
Assumptions are useful because they allow us to make quick decisions when information is incomplete. However, they can become obstacles when we treat them as unquestionable facts. In education, for instance, a learner who performs poorly on one test may still have considerable potential. Fair evaluation requires attention to context, progress, and evidence from more than one source.

# Level 3 — Pre-intermediate (41–60)

## Dictation 91 — Language and Identity
Language is more than a system for exchanging information; it is also closely connected to identity, memory, and belonging. The expressions people use may reflect their region, profession, age, or family background. When a language becomes less widely spoken, communities can lose access to stories and forms of knowledge that have been passed down for generations.

## Dictation 92 — The Ethics of Artificial Intelligence
Artificial intelligence can support decision-making in areas such as transport, education, and healthcare. Yet systems trained on incomplete or unbalanced data may produce unfair outcomes. Developers should explain how important decisions are made, test systems carefully, and provide ways for people to challenge errors. Technical performance alone is not enough to establish that a system is responsible.

## Dictation 93 — A Historical Turning Point
The introduction of the printing press transformed the circulation of information across Europe. Books could be produced more quickly and in greater numbers than before, allowing ideas to reach audiences beyond small groups of scholars. The change did not happen immediately, nor did it affect every community equally. Nevertheless, it contributed to major developments in education, religion, and public debate.

## Dictation 94 — The Research Findings
The research team examined whether short daily reading sessions could improve vocabulary retention among language learners. Participants were divided into two groups, and both groups completed the same assessment before and after the six-week programme. The results indicated a modest improvement among those who read regularly. However, the researchers warned that the sample was too small to support broad conclusions.

## Dictation 95 — Managing Uncertainty
People often prefer clear answers, even when the available evidence is incomplete. In professional settings, this preference can lead to decisions being made too quickly. A more responsible approach is to identify what is known, distinguish it from what is merely suspected, and explain where uncertainty remains. Acknowledging limitations does not weaken an argument; it can make the argument more credible.

## Dictation 96 — The Restoration Project
The restoration of the historic theatre required specialists from several fields. Architects examined the original structure, engineers assessed its safety, and conservators worked to protect the remaining decorative features. The project took eighteen months longer than planned because previously hidden damage was discovered behind the walls. Although the final cost exceeded the original estimate, the building can now be used by the public again.

## Dictation 97 — The Role of Education
Education should prepare people not only to pass examinations but also to respond thoughtfully to unfamiliar problems. Facts and technical skills remain important, yet learners also need to evaluate evidence, communicate clearly, and recognise different perspectives. These abilities develop gradually through practice, discussion, and meaningful feedback rather than through memorisation alone.

## Dictation 99 — Interpreting Evidence
A convincing explanation should account for the available evidence without ignoring information that challenges it. Researchers therefore compare alternative interpretations, examine the quality of their sources, and consider whether their methods could have influenced the results. Conclusions may need to be revised when new evidence becomes available. This is not necessarily a sign of failure; it is a normal part of careful investigation.

## Dictation 100 — The Long-Term Perspective
Meaningful progress is rarely the result of one extraordinary decision. More often, it develops through a series of deliberate actions that appear insignificant when considered separately. A student improves by practising consistently, a community changes through shared effort, and a research project advances through repeated testing and revision. Looking at the long-term process helps us recognise progress that immediate results might hide.

Before publishing a story, responsible journalists try to confirm important details. They may contact witnesses, compare documents, and ask experts to explain unfamiliar subjects.
